I was out of work for a while, six months. I was looking for work, six months. The job market was cautious, slow.

My friend clued me into a new opportunity, it honestly did not seem up my alley. The way he described it, it seemed like an experimental hack factory, a shop that is focused on churning out product ideas than any long term prospects. At the time I was looking for something more stable, some less 'seed'y. Unfortunately my job hunt was running long and the new mortgage was oppressive and so I was running out of options.

## It's not so bad

If there's something to be learned from every negative experience then I would say I was going back to school.

### Seeing Product failures in real time

This company was all in on AI. The product is AI, the leadership is AI, the culture is AI. Although I have a personal distaste for aggressive AI consumption and usage I don't blame them for relying so much on it. We needed to move fast and see what stuck. There's not much time to spend thinking about what to build but rather to just build it. I find this form of development short-sighted and careless. Decisions are made being reactively to Claude produced stimulus rather than being thoughtful about what customers want versus what they say they want. Historically, Product teams needed to wait for a whole development cycle to see the results of their hypothesis, now they can see the results in near real time. This is good in that we're able to validate faster, but it also drains the backlog faster. There's pressure to insert more ideas and none are truly thought out, just simply reactive. Implmentation costs being infinitely cheaper, requires infinitely cheap ideas to feed into the pipeline in kind lest the pipeline goes dry. Cheap ideas are formed from cheap thoughts. Regardless of how cheap implementation has become, it is not appropriate to proportionately reduce the effort of thinking to take full advantage of the throughput of implementation.

Of course I'm not suggesting to take a quarter to think of the product vision. There is a balance here. A balance to find ideas that have a good chance of hitting vs missing.

### Have your problem be personal

It was so apparent that nobody on our team had any understanding or association or experience in the problem we were trying to solve. We were foreigners. strangers in a strange land trying to understand the customs and culture to solve a conflict that has been plaguing the area. We talked with the locals, we even hired one for inhouse council but we could not get our vision to be accepted. Nobody wanted to use our product.

The hubris we had to think that we could create tooling that creatives would want to use when none of us had a creative bone in our body. None of us produced film, none of us wrote a screenplay, a story, and yet we thought we could create a professional filmmaking tool. We created the software, it looked like traditional film making software, but for those in the industry can see, it was just a facsimile of familiar concepts. It's an imitation of something that looks useful. It's slop.

### The slop must flow

Finding balance comes from knowing the extremes. Or in more computer science terms, binary search requires you to know your min and max. In order to find a balance you have to know what the extremes are. Throughout my entire time here I was always adjusting the slider either towards or away from vibe slop or refinement. I've seen projects slopped together, bandaid after bandaid makes it hard to understand what the original injuries are. I've seen projects that are 'cleanly' structured, hard to move forward for fear of creating the wrong abstraction. I first started with my slider biased towards cleanliness and understanding, but by the end of my stay it leans more towards vibe. The majority of features was written by a person with no prior web application engineering experience. Code not reviewed by me is often approved within seconds, no comments. Identified issues post merge never get time to be cleaned up. Despite acting as the sole custodian of the code base, I recognized the futility of trying to keep the grounds clean and wondered at what point the messiness would come to bite us. It's been around six months and around 1000prs and this moment has not yet arrived.

It is hard to detangle whether the wall never arrived because the platform was set up for success, whether I was doing an acceptable job maintaining the grass, whether the models got better, whether the models are good enough. Does it matter that the garden is unkempt? Now that I'm gone I may never know. I'll ask my friend who still works there that when the company goes under in six more months but I'm skeptical that there would be any difference in development quality of life or velocity and I suspect that my insistence on clean code was all in vain.
