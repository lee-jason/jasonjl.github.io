The new world has entered faster than anyone could have anticipated. The software engineer who once held the sacred texts and the secret incantations to turn coal ino gold are losing relevance by the second. Now the blacksmiths, the weavers, the farmers, and clerics are all empowered to perform their own alchemy. Where does this leave the software engineers?

Every few days I encounter a scenario which makes me rethink the value I bring to the company. I'm producing at a rate many multiples than I used to pre-AI, but on the surface, this doesn't seem to be any more valuable than someone with access to parallel agents and a minor affliction of ADHD. I'm able to review code using the breadth of my learned experiences, but so what? Agents are able to do this thoroughly and quickly with half hearted prompt of 'review this code'. I'm able to think of product ideas and write up fleshed out requirements but once again, AI is able to do this with a flurry of product ideas with full requirements in a minute. So, now it sounds like the AI is better in every way at doing the job of a Software Engineer than the human ever was, and to some extent it is Writing code, running tests, validating, deploying, it can do it all. So, 'What would you say you do here?'.

It is the engineer's responsibility to ensure that everything keeps running and everything stays buildable. At some point of in development software teams encounter 'The Wall' and it is the engineering team's job to manage The Wall while also building it. 'The Wall' is anything that makes building software difficult. It's untested code, its hacks, its performance issues, its outdated dependencies, its anti-patterns, its unreviewed code, its features. Some teams call it tech debt. I like to think of it as a wall.

### The Wall

You start a fresh repo, your wall is non-existant. You build your first feature, then your next. You pick frameworks and libraries with impunity. You feel unstoppable. You release feature after feature. You're flying back and forth through an opening between fences, your movement unimpeded. Your first prospective customer asks if you can integrate directly to their proprietary data format. You agree, You notice a mound growing beneath your feet, you gently step over it as you walk through an opening in the fence line. You hire a few engineers to help build out features for your customer, everyone is pretty responsible but in a display to show your first client how fast you build you deploy new features untested. There's a small boulder in your way, you take a big step over it for every decision you make. Your feature list grows but your access patterns get sloppy, db lookups get sluggish, you apply a few conditional paths to access data slightly differently in special cases. Your boulder is getting larger, it's getting harder to climb over it, but you can barely squeeze through it. Your team adopts AI first development. Features are flying through the door, decisions are being offloaded to the AI. After a few fast months it starts getting harder to make changes to the system, its incomprehensible and no one understands how it works confidently enough to want to change it. You notice your path is blocked with a The Wall.

The Wall is a concept applicable to both AI development and traditional development. It only gets built faster without anyone noticing at a much faster rate. The accelerated rate of development with AI also accelerates the rate at which The Wall gets built. It is inevitable, it is unavoidable, the best thing we can do is manage it in the same way that we've managed it before the age of AI. It is easy in the age of AI to be complacent with the solutions that AI proposes, but it is more important now than ever before to understand what you're releasing. Understanding what you're building is the first step to acknowledging how much of The Wall exists and how best to dismantle it. It is tempting to give AI the reigns on decision making, and it is even more tempting to place blind trust in what its generating, but it is critical to not get complacent, otherwise, what value would you bring if I can just ask the AI?

### Judgement

So what good is understanding the code if you're not going to be applying your judgement? Judgement is taste. Judgement comes from years of experience of seeing how software is built, what worked, what didn't, what needs addressing now, what can wait for the future. AI can make judgement calls, they can be correct, they probably will be correct, but despite AI's ability to scour an infinite amount of context, they cannot scan your memories, your design talk with your teammate earlier that week, your shower thoughts, that blog you read a month ago. All of these experiences shape your judgement and decision making that'll make a better decision than the AI can ever make. Not because the AI is incapable, but because its missing context. Until it gains that context it will most likely make a suboptimal decision
